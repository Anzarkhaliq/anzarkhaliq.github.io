How I Think About Learning

The frameworks I have built for designing courses, with examples of how faculty use them.

Most of what I know about teaching has been learned slowly, in classrooms in Karachi and California, and in many conversations with faculty trying to make a course work. Over time those lessons settled into a small set of frameworks. Each one is a tool for making a design decision, and each one answers a single question.

At SFBU these frameworks now sit inside a 54-page faculty guide that my team and I wrote at CEPI, Teaching and Learning at SFBU. The guide is written for SFBU faculty, so this page shares the key parts for a wider audience. If you would like to see the full guide or talk about using these frameworks with your own faculty, write to me.

For the full guide or a conversation about these frameworks, write to anzar.khaliq@sfbu.edu.

Framework 01

The Pyramid After AI

What should students still do themselves once AI can produce the first draft?

For most of the last century, a finished essay or a correct answer was reasonable evidence that a student had done the thinking. Generative AI has broken that link. A student can now hand in a fluent, well-structured piece of work in minutes, and the work tells a teacher very little about what the student understands.

The Pyramid After AI is my answer to that problem. It sits upside down on top of Bloom’s Taxonomy, meeting it at the point where AI output becomes the starting point of a student’s work. From there, six human capacities rise. Students use them in cycles and return to earlier ones as their understanding grows.

1Decode

Do I understand the output?

2Judge

Is it good, true or appropriate?

3Shape

Does it make sense, and have I reshaped it?

4Author

Does my thinking, voice and judgment show?

5Transfer

Can I apply this learning in a new situation?

6Become

What changed in me as a learner or human being?

In practice

A psychology student reads an AI-generated summary of trauma research and notices that it has merged acute and chronic trauma, a distinction that matters in clinical work. Catching that error is Decoding, and it takes knowledge the model does not have.

Framework 02

Think / Do / Create

How should one idea travel through a session, a unit or a course?

Learning often stalls at understanding. A student can explain a concept, pass the exam and still be unable to use the idea anywhere else. Alfred North Whitehead called these inert ideas in 1929. Later research points the same way: students learn more when they build and interact with material than when they only receive it (Chi and Wylie, 2014).

Think / Do / Create is the smallest design unit I have found that avoids this. Students meet and process an idea (Think), work with it in a real setting (Do), then produce something that did not exist before they met it (Create). The same arc works for a fifty-minute session, a three-week unit and a whole course.

In practice

A unit that asks students only to read and absorb leaves application to chance. A unit that begins with Create asks students to build without materials. Marking each activity in a week as Think, Do or Create shows a teacher where the course is out of balance.

Framework 03

PERS

What should every learning experience feel like?

PERS is the shared language I helped shape for teaching at SFBU. It names four qualities that any session, assignment or piece of feedback can carry: Personalized, Experiential, Relationship-Rich and Student-Centered. All four are present in every class. Faculty choose whether each one is designed on purpose or left to chance.

In practice

A unit on supply chain disruption could open with the same textbook case for everyone. It could also invite students to bring disruptions they have seen in their home regions or family businesses. The concepts stay the same, and a student who watched a family business deal with port closures in South Asia brings knowledge no textbook case can supply.

Framework 04

The New Learning Cycle

What path does one assignment take now that AI is in the room?

The old cycle of an assignment was short: receive it, produce the work, submit it, get a grade. With AI in the room, that cycle can be completed without much learning at all.

The new cycle starts where AI starts. Students Prompt and Generate, then Doubt what came back and Argue with it. They Shape it to a real situation, Reflect on what they did and Recreate the work in their own voice. Assessment follows the judgment behind the product: the choices a student made, the options they rejected and the reasoning they can defend.

Framework 05

Regulate, Relate, Reason

What has to be true before a student can think hard?

This sequence comes from Bruce Perry’s neurosequential model (Perry and Winfrey, 2021), and I use it as a design condition for every course. The brain has to feel safe before it can connect with others. It has to connect before it can reason at the level a university course demands. Stress can impair the prefrontal cortex, which supports complex reasoning (Arnsten, 2009).

For teaching, that means predictable structure first. Next comes a class culture where students are taken seriously even when they are wrong. The demanding intellectual work comes third. A surprise quiz costs more than it seems, because it lands hardest on students who already carry the most stress.

Framework 06

Relational Intelligence

Who will this student know at the end of the course that they do not know today?

Relational intelligence is the ability to build and keep working relationships, across disagreement and difference. My working claim is that faculty can teach it and assess it. It includes four capacities: building trust, holding disagreement, repairing a relationship after it breaks and working across difference.

The most direct tool is the relationship-rich assignment. Every assignment already creates interactions, usually with a search bar, a rubric and a midnight deadline. A relationship-rich design redirects one of those interactions toward a person, so the student cannot finish the work without them. The design draws on Felten and Lambert’s work on relationship-rich education (2020). It also draws on a trial across 22 institutions, in which a brief belonging intervention raised first-year persistence (Walton, Murphy and colleagues, 2023).

Framework 07

CPACT

What does a whole course need, beyond its content?

CPACT is the first framework I built, at Habib University, to help faculty think about a course as a whole. It asks a designer to plan five things together: content, pedagogy, assessment, community and transdisciplinarity. Many of the later frameworks grew out of the gaps CPACT made visible, especially the place of community and relationships in a course.

How They Fit Together

Each framework works at a different level. PERS says how learning should feel, and Think / Do / Create says how to structure it. Bloom’s and the Pyramid After AI say what thinking to ask for. The New Learning Cycle carries one assignment through that thinking, and Regulate, Relate, Reason says what must be in place first.
