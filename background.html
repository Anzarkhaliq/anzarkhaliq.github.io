From the Beamline to the Classroom

I trained in physics, hosted a radio show and performed improvisation. Each one shaped how I design learning.

Design, Improvisation and Human Learning

I have always liked different forms of expression and different ways of seeing the world. As a physics student, I studied by day and hosted a rock and metal radio show at night. During my doctorate in experimental physics, I began formal training in theater and improvisation.

I kept working across these worlds after I began teaching and research. Each one taught me something about people, communication, creativity and learning.

Physics trained me to think convergently, with analysis, numbers, system and precision. Theater, improvisation and radio trained a different mode of attention. That mode is built on narrative, audience, uncertainty, listening, timing and presence.

Storytelling asks for divergent thinking. Improvisation asks for adaptability and quick response. Radio asks for attention to mood, pacing and the audience. Without planning it, I carried these habits into my classrooms, my facilitation and my learning design.

These experiences made me attentive to user experience before I met design thinking as a formal method. I later saw that storytelling, improvisation and performance share foundations with experiential learning, facilitation and human-centered design.

Improvisation trained me to anticipate how people will experience a learning environment. When I design, I plan for responsiveness, failure points, emotional dynamics and participation. I also plan for the unexpected ways people will use what I build.

Today my work combines systems thinking with close attention to people. I study lived experience and institutional structures together. I design learning environments that are participatory, reflective, creative and adaptive.

AI, Systems Thinking and Higher Education

I design educational systems that keep growing after the person who started them leaves. My work covers single courses and teaching practices. It also covers the cultures, frameworks and structures that help an institution reflect, adapt and improve over time.

At Habib University I built several connected initiatives in teaching, learning and transdisciplinary education. I founded the Integrated Sciences and Mathematics program and the Center for Pedagogical Excellence. I created the R3 Teaching Talks series and the Teaching Excellence Awards.

I also developed the CPACT framework for course design. CPACT stands for content, pedagogy, assessment, community and transdisciplinarity. With colleagues I co-founded The Playground, a lab for human-centered design, experimentation and interdisciplinary problem-solving.

Many of these systems are still running and growing at Habib. That experience shaped my view that an institution improves through shared cultures and structures. Individual brilliance cannot sustain that growth on its own.

At SFBU, one of my first projects gave the teaching goals of the university a shared language. That language is the PERS model: Personalized, Experiential, Relationship-Rich and Student-Centered learning.

I also study how AI can change teaching, learning, faculty development, instructional design and educational systems. I use human-centered AI frameworks to support reflection, creativity, metacognition, participation and dialogue. Productivity is one use among many.

This work includes AI-supported faculty development programs and custom teaching assistants. It includes AI in course design and delivery, and AI-supported models for classroom engagement. I also test new forms of instructional design that go past static content.

I am interested in where AI meets interface design and student experience. AI can reduce friction in a learning environment and help students find their way through complex university systems. In many experiments, AI helps learners notice their own assumptions, thinking patterns, decisions and ways of making meaning.

My recent work prototypes new educational products and learning ecosystems built on human-centered AI principles. Automation has a supporting part in them. The work re-examines instructional design, assessment, participation and feedback, and it asks how people and intelligent systems should relate in a learning environment.

How can educational systems stay human while they respond to technological, cultural and social change?

Ongoing Explorations

Some of my current questions about higher education excite me, and some unsettle me.

I continue to study trauma-informed teaching, neurodivergence, community cultural wealth and culturally sustaining practices. I treat them as foundations for learning environments that are humane, participatory and responsive to students.

I also study how AI is changing basic assumptions about education. AI systems now act as collaborators, co-designers and reflective partners in learning environments. Many traditional structures in education look unstable as a result.

This raises questions about assessment, curriculum design and human learning. In Bloom’s Taxonomy, creation is the last stage of learning. What happens when creation becomes the first stage? What would a redesigned taxonomy look like when information and generated text are abundant?

I want to make the hidden curriculum visible. I treat durable human skills as central outcomes of educational design. I ask how out-of-class learning, experimentation, reflection, collaboration and lived experience can become part of formal learning.

I also ask why courses are isolated from one another. Most curricula run as separate silos, and each course knows little about the others. AI makes it possible to design courses that are connected, adaptive and aware of each other. Such courses could change continuously and keep their content current.

These questions call for a larger redesign of learning experiences, institutional systems and the relationship between people and intelligent technologies. Parts of this period in higher education feel uncertain and even frightening. I am fortunate to work with colleagues and institutions who are willing to experiment and question assumptions.

I believe higher education can still become more human, reflective, connected and meaningful. I am glad to take part in that work, and I hope some of these experiments point to one possible direction.
