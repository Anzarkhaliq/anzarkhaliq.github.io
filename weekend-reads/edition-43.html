Ten Minutes That Clear a Tired Class’s Head

 
Energy fades in the second half of every semester, for students and for us. Research points to a few small acts that help.

Dear Colleagues,

Welcome to the 43rd edition of Weekend Reads from CEPI.

Picture your class on a Tuesday in early November. The same students are there, in the same seats. In September, hands went up before you finished the question. Now the pause after a question runs long, and the answer comes from the same two students. A pair discussion that once filled ten minutes is done in four. Twenty minutes before the end, someone checks the clock, and a few bags are zipped before your last slide. If someone suggested ending early, the room would agree at once.

You may have noticed the same thing in yourself. The class you prepared with care in September now gets prepared on Monday night, and you pour the second coffee earlier in the day.

Before reading on, place a bet. Does this drop in energy set in during one bad week, or does it build from the first weeks of term?

For years most of his class time has gone to his own slides, and it has served him well. Lately he has wondered whether his students need something different, so he has started reading these letters with his Saturday coffee.

  A Semester That Fades Slowly

A team in the United Kingdom asked 175 undergraduates every week how often they did seventeen learning activities, from attending class to using the library.[1] Over the term every activity declined. Effort rose only in weeks with an assessment, then fell back.

So the fatigue has been building since the first assessment. Deadlines from every course stack up across the same weeks, and by early November it shows in the room. This Sunday the clocks go back an hour, and the evenings turn dark as well.

The pattern the research reports, drawn as an illustration.[1] It is not SFBU data. Darker means more energy in the room.

In focus groups at two British universities, students said they could see their teachers’ energy and mood.[2] They described a cycle in which a quiet class drains the teacher, and a tired teacher makes the class quieter still.

 

  Four Paths for the Last Five Weeks

Each path keeps the standard where it is and gives tired students a clearer way to meet it. Pick one for the coming week.

1

Tell them you see it, and show the plan

When this week
Time about thirty minutes

Send one short message to the class. Say that you know this point in the term is tiring, and what you will do about it. Promise no surprise assignments, break larger tasks into steps, and keep class time for the parts students find hardest. Attach a one-page map of the weeks that remain, with every due date on it. End with “Tell me what would help.”

Research
The closest evidence is on clear assignments. When faculty spelled out the purpose, task and criteria of their assignments, students did better, especially first-generation, low-income and underrepresented students.[3]

2

Ten minutes to clear and plan

When early next week, and again before finals
Time ten minutes of class

Students spend two minutes writing down everything on their minds. Then they list what is due in all their courses over the next two weeks, and choose a first step and a time for each. At tables of four, they compare plans and borrow each other’s ideas.

Research
Writing about worries just before an important test raised exam scores, most of all for anxious students.[4] Across 94 studies, deciding in advance when, where and how to act made people more likely to follow through on their goals.[5]

3

Five minutes of recall

When the start of every class until finals
Time five minutes

Put three questions about last week’s material on the board. Students answer from memory, with no notes and no grade, then compare answers at their tables before you reveal anything.

Research
A review of 50 experiments in real classrooms found that no-stakes recall improved learning across subjects and ages, with medium or large benefits in most.[6]

4

A week that uses all three

When the coming week
Time about an hour in total

Send the message and the map on Monday. Give ten minutes to clearing and planning on Tuesday. Open every class after that with five minutes of recall.

  A Care Package for the Second Half

For you

The second half of the semester is also when grading stacks up on the kitchen table and the light goes early. These four habits help the person at the front of the room.

Take short breaks between tasks.

Across 22 studies, breaks of ten minutes or less raised people’s energy and reduced their fatigue.[7] A walk to the food truck by the Student Success Hub on a Tuesday or Thursday counts.

Switch off on the weekend.

Across 91 groups and more than 38,000 workers, people who mentally detached from work in their time off reported less exhaustion and fatigue.[8] Keep one weekend day free of email and grading if you can.

Find colleagues who are in it too.

At UC Berkeley, a faculty learning community on disability access names exhaustion and isolation among the problems it addresses.[9] Its remedy is a group of colleagues meeting monthly through the year. If a few of you want to meet about anything in this letter, write to cepi@sfbu.edu and we will set it up.

Know where support is.

CrisisNow, TimelyCare’s around-the-clock support line, is open to all SFBU students and employees. Call (510) 340-4361. The massage chairs in the Student Success Hub are open to students, faculty and staff.
