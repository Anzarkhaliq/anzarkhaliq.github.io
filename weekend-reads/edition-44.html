Why Students Forget What the Chatbot Explained

Many students study for finals with a chatbot open. What they do before they open it affects how much they remember.

This week at a glance

The idea  Students remember more when they write from memory before they ask a chatbot.

Try  Ten minutes of writing from memory, then the tool.

Looking ahead  What changes when AI is always on.

Dear Colleagues,

Welcome to the 44th edition of Weekend Reads from CEPI.

Picture a student in your course. It is 11 p.m., three weeks before the final. Her review sheet is open in one window and a chatbot in the other. She pastes in the first topic and asks for an explanation. The answer is clear and well organized. She reads it, nods, and pastes in the next topic. By midnight she has gone through the whole sheet.

Now picture her classmate, with the same sheet and the same chatbot. Before he opens the chatbot, he spends ten minutes writing down everything he remembers about each topic. Then he asks the chatbot only about the gaps.

Scenes like these are common. On Freakonomics Radio last month, Dr. Ethan Mollick of the Wharton School said that about 60 percent of students in surveys say they use AI.[1] They also believe that 90 percent of the people around them use it.

The missed question showed Theo that his twenty minutes of slides had left little behind. He decided to teach that idea again, this time with a short problem students solve at their tables.

  What Stays After a Week

We have results on these two habits from a study twenty years ago. Dr. Henry Roediger and Dr. Jeffrey Karpicke of Washington University in St. Louis gave 120 students a short passage to study.[2] Some students then read it again. Others wrote down what they could remember.

Five minutes later, the students who reread remembered more. A week later, the students who wrote from memory kept 56 percent of the ideas. The rereaders kept 42 percent.

Reading a chatbot’s explanation is close to rereading, and writing first is a form of recall. Two recent studies look at this question with AI.

At the MIT Media Lab, Dr. Nataliya Kosmyna and colleagues asked 54 adults to write essays over several sessions.[3] Some used ChatGPT, and some used no tools. In the fourth session, the two groups switched.

People who wrote on their own first and then used ChatGPT remembered more of what they wrote. Their brain recordings also showed more activity. People who started with ChatGPT and then wrote without it showed less activity. Only 18 people took part in that session, and the study has not yet been peer reviewed. We treat it as an early sign.

A larger study points the same way. Dr. Hamsa Bastani of the Wharton School and colleagues studied nearly a thousand high school math students in Turkey.[4] Students who practiced with a regular chatbot scored 48 percent higher on practice problems. On the exam, without the chatbot, they scored 17 percent lower than students who never had it. A chatbot built to give hints and hold back answers mostly prevented that drop.

So the order matters, and so does what students ask the tool to do. These were high school students, so the result is only a guide for our adult students. Both AI studies still point to the same habit, which is to try first and ask second.

 

  Three Ways to Put Thinking First

Each takes a few minutes and works in any subject. Pick one for the next two weeks.

1  Ten minutes from memory, then the tool

When your next review sessionTime ten minutes

Give students the list of topics for the final. They pick one topic and write everything they remember, with notes closed and screens down. Then they open any tool they like, check what they missed, and mark each gap in a different color.

Research
Students who wrote from memory remembered more a week later than students who reread.[2] In the Wharton study, gains made with a regular chatbot disappeared on the exam.[4]

2  Ask the tool to quiz you

When this weekTime five minutes to explain

Show students how to turn a chatbot into a quiz master. One example prompt is “Ask me one question at a time about the causes of inflation, and wait for my answer before you explain.” Ask students to bring three of its questions, with their answers, to the next class.

Research
In the Wharton study, the chatbot that gave hints and held back answers protected exam scores.[4]

3  A week that uses both

When the coming weekTime fifteen minutes in total

On Tuesday, give ten minutes of writing from memory before the review. On Thursday, start class with the questions students brought from their quiz master, and answer the hardest one together.

  A Preview of the Future

Everything above assumes a student can choose when to open a chatbot. That choice may not last. In June, Kevin Kelly, a co-founder of Wired, predicted that most people will carry an AI that is always on.[5] He expects young adults to go first. The AI will listen, remember what we say and do, and get to know us well. He calls it an exoself.

Kelly describes four relationships we might have with it. It can be a twin that thinks like us, or a guardian whose judgment we follow. It can be an assistant that works within limits while we stay in charge, or a friend that brings out our best.

Look at the Wharton study again with these in mind. Students with a regular chatbot leaned on it, in the authors’ words, like a crutch, and their exam scores fell. In Kelly’s terms, they treated it as a guardian. Trying first keeps the student in charge, with the AI as her assistant.

When AI is always on, that habit is what we can still teach. A student’s exoself will only be as good as the questions she asks it and the answers she checks. We will come back to this idea in the coming weeks, starting with our own exoselves as faculty.

Which of Kelly’s four relationships do you want your students to have with AI? Which one do you have today?
