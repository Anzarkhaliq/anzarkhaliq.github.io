01 Educational Experiments

Educational Experiments

Five courses designed to be transdisciplinary, experiential and reflective.

Courses

  4. How to Communicate in a Global Context

Many human problems do not fit inside one academic department. Much of higher education still divides knowledge into separate silos. Over time I grew dissatisfied with teaching that stays inside one discipline.

The courses on this page are my attempts to design learning that is transdisciplinary, experiential and reflective. I designed many of them in response to AI. Universities can no longer rely on old assumptions about content, assessment, expertise and learning.

These courses focus on metacognition, systems thinking, creativity, reflection, dialogue and experimentation. Most do not use a standard textbook. Students learn through active inquiry, lived experience, interdisciplinary work and AI-supported processes.

01

How Can We Thrive? Scientific Inquiry and the Future of Sustainability

APP 301 · San Francisco Bay University · Teaching now, Fall 2026

Teaching now · Fall 2026

I am teaching this course at SFBU in Fall 2026. Before class each week, every student holds a one-on-one conversation with Bayley. Bayley is an AI video companion built with Alma Learning around that week’s sustainability question. It asks each student to take a position, defend it and answer the strongest objection.

The conversations produce a class report that I receive every Monday. The report shows where students agree, where their reasoning is thin and which scientific ideas need a second look. I redesign the Tuesday session from that report, so class time goes to the questions students could not settle alone.

The course treats sustainability as one connected challenge with human, technological, ecological, economic and ethical parts. It draws on the physical and biological sciences. Students ask how natural systems work, how human systems disrupt or support them and which futures are still possible.

Systems thinking runs through every unit. Students study renewable energy, sustainable materials, biodiversity and climate change. They also study circular economies, sustainable agriculture, ecosystem services, nanotechnology and bio-inspired design.

In each topic, students weigh environmental integrity, economic viability and social equity. The course gives justice, context, trade-offs and long-term responsibility the same weight as scientific feasibility.

Most class time goes to dialogue and practice, with less time on content delivery. Students treat scientific inquiry as a social process. That process involves debate, interpretation, uncertainty and shared meaning-making.

I am writing a three-act book for the course, How Can We Thrive? It brings together scientific, philosophical, technological, ecological, political and human views of sustainability.

AI has four jobs in the course. It supports metacognition, preparation, participation and scientific dialogue. Students still do the thinking themselves.

Before each class, students prepare for a parliamentary-style debate on a major sustainability question. They also debate the question one-on-one with Bayley, which is trained on that week’s topic. Students take positions, defend arguments and meet competing views before they arrive.

In class, students look at patterns across the whole set of debates. They read AI-generated summaries of the scientific arguments and find gaps in understanding. Together we work through the ideas that need more scientific explanation, and then we plan action.

Students also connect each global question to their own local and cultural context. Regions, economies and communities experience environmental problems and solutions in different ways.

The course treats sustainability as a design challenge that keeps changing. Students need scientific literacy, systems thinking, creativity and ethical reflection to meet it. They also need to imagine and prototype more sustainable futures.

02

How to Design Your Life: A Journey Through Personal Epistemology

San Francisco Bay University

I designed this course out of concern about how universities treat students. Many models hand students pre-packaged knowledge and keep learning apart from real human experience.

The course is built on personal epistemology, the way a person comes to understand knowledge, belief, identity, experience and meaning. Students study how they build their understanding of themselves, of other people and of the world.

The students’ own lived experience is a central part of the course. Through the semester they examine their assumptions, relationships, influences, fears and priorities. They also examine how their ways of making meaning change.

The course uses design thinking, reflection, dialogue and experiential learning. Students think about the lives they are designing and the systems that shape those lives.

Students run small personal experiments through the semester. They reflect on the people and experiences that shaped their beliefs. They examine the stories they inherited about themselves, and they start to choose the stories they want to live by.

The course asks for small actions that a student can sustain, and it sets aside grand life plans. These actions help students live in a more intentional and self-directed way.

I designed the course for the AI era. Metacognition, reflection, human complexity and personal agency sit at its center.

03

How to Communicate in a Global Context

San Francisco Bay University

Ideas, technologies, products, media and people cross borders every day. Many courses still teach communication from inside one narrow cultural context. I designed this course in response.

Students study how meaning changes across cultures. They look at how communication styles, expectations, values, power and social norms shape the way an idea is received. A message that feels natural or respectful in one place can read very differently in another.

Students learn to notice Western-centered assumptions in their own communication. They study how people in much of the world experience relationships, authority, collaboration, feedback, identity and expression.

The course is built on practice. Students take part in simulations, group activities, role-plays and design challenges. Each one puts them inside an intercultural communication scenario.

AI generates the simulations and cross-cultural scenarios. Students test their communication against the expectations of different countries and settings.

The assessments go beyond exams. In one escape-room-style midterm, students adapt a product pitch for representatives from several cultures. They advance to the next room only when the pitch works for that audience.

The course follows the SFBU approach called Think, Do, Create. It combines critical reflection, practical experiments and creative problem-solving. Students treat communication as a human practice tied to culture, identity, empathy, systems and lived experience.

04

Scientific Methods

Habib Liberal Core Curriculum, Habib University

I was the principal designer of Scientific Methods, a course in the Habib Liberal Core Curriculum. The course has three arcs. The first covers the history of scientific thought, the second covers the practice of inquiry and the third applies scientific techniques in projects.

One question runs under all three arcs. How do people come to understand the natural world, and how does that understanding change over time?

The first arc traces scientific thought across civilizations, traditions and revolutions. Gravity is the thread. Students follow it from Greek natural philosophy to Muslim scientific scholarship and the Renaissance. They continue through the observations of Galileo and Newtonian mechanics to the account of space, time and gravity given by Einstein.

Students learn that scientific knowledge changes. A paradigm can look stable from the inside and still be open to challenge and revision. Students study how claims become stable, how paradigms weaken and how new worldviews appear.

Students read Popper, Feynman and Sagan. They discuss positivism, determinism, probability, free will, cosmology, time and the nature of inquiry. Questions such as “Where do we come from?” and “What existed before the Big Bang?” open discussions about the reach and the limits of science.

The second arc teaches the practice of inquiry and the logic of the scientific method. Students form hypotheses, run experiments, observe and interpret. They study how evidence, models, uncertainty and explanation relate to each other.

The third arc covers scientific techniques and project work. Students use statistics, data analysis, modeling and investigation in research projects of their own.

Students show their understanding in many forms. They make visual novels, comics, podcasts, performances and role-plays about science. Exams and research papers are one part of the assessment.

Students have wide freedom to experiment with ideas and forms. They also study the history, philosophy, methods and practice of science in depth.

05

Energy

Habib University · First taught in 2014

I designed this course in 2014. At the time, Karachi and the rest of Pakistan faced severe energy shortages that everyone could see. The course treats energy as a scientific, political, ecological, economic, ethical and human question.

The course also responds to the Anthropocene and climate change. Pakistan was in a hard position. As a developing country it needed more energy quickly, and it faced the costs of fossil fuels in an unstable climate.

The course is cross-disciplinary. Each arc studies energy through a different field.

The first arc is physics. Students study solar, wind, hydroelectric and nuclear energy, along with the physics of producing and transferring energy.

The second arc is chemistry. Students study geoengineering, carbon capture, batteries, energy storage and the chemistry of climate intervention.

The third arc is biology. Students study biofuels, bioenergy, bio-inspired design, ecosystems and the link between energy and living things.

The fourth arc is policy and governance. Students compare the energy policy of Pakistan with regional and global approaches. They ask hard questions about infrastructure, regulation, equity, political decisions and system failures. They judge what was technically possible and what was happening politically, ethically and socially around them.

The final arc covers communication, awareness and human behavior. Students study how societies talk about energy, consumption, climate change and ecological crisis. Topics include the two-degree Celsius threshold, the energy use of the internet and the sixth mass extinction.

Learning in the course reached past the classroom into public action. Students designed campaigns, communication materials and awareness projects for families, communities and social networks. They published the work on social media.

Part of each grade came from the conversations and impact the work produced outside the university. The course asked for scientific literacy, ethical reflection, policy awareness, communication, creativity and public engagement.
