Five Weeks to a Fairer Final

Finals are five weeks away. Spreading out review and designing the final with neurodivergent students in mind can help more students show what they know.

This week at a glance

The idea  Review spread over several weeks, and a final designed for students who think differently.

Try  Three questions from earlier weeks at the start of class, and a clear picture of the final shared this week.

Time  Five minutes a class, plus twenty minutes once.

  What the Final Measures

A team at Coventry University in England reviewed 48 studies of neurodivergent students in higher education, most of them about dyslexia, autism or ADHD.[1] On exams, the studies point to extra time, a room without distractions, and exams spread out with rest days between them.

The review also found that written work, such as the essay, still dominates. A focus on grammar, spelling and punctuation can penalize dyslexic students. The authors recommend other forms of assessment that test the same learning.

Individual adjustments help, but some students dislike being identified and labeled by them, and some never ask for support because they fear the label. The authors also found that staff responses were mixed, and that support was not always consistent.

The authors propose building many of these ideas into every course, for all students. Many are simple to put in place, they write, and could benefit all students. Giving every student more flexibility, they suggest, might be fairer.

In our view, clear instructions and more than one way to show learning remove some barriers before anyone has to ask. Students may still need individual accommodations. The same changes may also help the student reading in her third language and the one watching the clock.

 

  Coming Back to Each Topic

Clear instructions help on the day of the final. Students also need several chances, in the weeks before, to come back to the ideas the final asks them to use. Researchers at the University of California, San Diego, led by Dr. Nicholas Cepeda and Dr. Harold Pashler, combined 317 experiments on how people study.[2] On later tests, people generally recalled more after study spread over separate sessions than after the same study time in one session.

The best gap grew with the time until the test, and most experiments used tests only days or weeks away. For a final five weeks out, a week between visits to a topic is a reasonable guess.

In a study at Tufts University, researchers taught 120 students a set of words and pictures, either by restudying them or by taking practice tests.[3] A day later, half gave an unexpected speech in front of judges before recalling the set. Stressed students who had restudied recalled about 7 of 30 items, and those who had practiced testing recalled about 11.

Last week’s letter described students who wrote from memory and recalled more a week later.[4] We suggest spreading that recall across the coming weeks, so every student has several chances to use the ideas before the final. No one is then left with a whole course to relearn in the last few days.

  Four Changes for the Last Five Weeks

Choose the ones that fit your final, whether it is an exam, a project or a presentation. The first two can start this week.

1  Three questions from earlier weeks

When the start of every class until finalsTime five minutes

Open each class with three questions, one from this week and two from earlier weeks. Students answer from memory, then compare at their tables. Rotate the older topics so each one comes back every week or two.

Research
Across 317 experiments, people generally recalled more after spaced study than after one long session.[2]

2  Show students what the final will ask for

When this weekTime twenty minutes

Tell students how long the final is, what it asks them to produce, what they may bring or use, and how marks are split. Add one sample question, or one strong example of a past project with its rubric.

Research
Some students in the review never asked for support, for fear of the label.[1] Sharing the final with everyone is our own suggestion.

3  Hold a practice round

When two weeks before finalsTime twenty minutes

For an exam, give a short practice test in the same format. For a project or presentation, hold a checkpoint where each student explains one design choice to their table. Nothing is graded. Note what most of the room found hardest, and teach that again.

Research
In the Tufts study, students who had practiced with tests recalled more words and pictures after a stressful task.[3] The project checkpoint is our own suggestion.

4  One task at a time

When as you write the final or the project briefTime about an hour

Break long questions and long briefs into short sentences, one task each, and number the parts. Then set the time so a student who knows the material can finish without hurrying, unless speed is part of what you are assessing.

Research
The review lists extra time among the exam adjustments that help neurodivergent students.[1] Short, single-task instructions are CEPI’s own recommendation.

Students with approved accommodations work with Student Accessibility Services, at accessibility@sfbu.edu. If you would like a second reader for your final or your project brief, write to cepi@sfbu.edu.

  Weekend Prompt

This weekend, open your final exam or project brief in an AI tool your institution has approved. Paste in the final with this request. “A student with dyslexia or ADHD who learned English as a second language may take this final. Mark every sentence that asks for more than one task, every word they may not know, and every instruction that could be read two ways. And for each one, explain in a sentence why it could slow the student down.”

The list points to changes that would help a range of students, and the explanations give you words for why. Over time, you build your own vocabulary for these changes, and you may start to catch them as you write your next final.
